UNEQUAL EXCHANGE OF LABOUR IN THE WORLD ECONOMY

The Unequal exchange of labour in the world economy is a pressing issue that has been ongoing for decades. At its core, unequal exchange refers to the systemic transfer of labour and ecological value from poor countries in the global South to rich countries in the global North. This phenomenon is rooted in the structural inequalities of the global economy, where wages and natural resource prices are significantly lower in the South than in the North. As a result, poor countries are forced to export large quantities of labour and resources to achieve a monetary balance of trade, leading to a constant transfer of value from the periphery to the core as mentioned in the dependency theory of development. This process has been exacerbated by imperialist policies, including the suppression of Southern wages and prices, and the preservation of Northern monopoly power.

The recent findings are staggering: the South has lost a whopping $152 trillion, equivalent to 97% of its 2017 GDP. Instead of reinvesting this surplus, it has been transferred to the North, contributing to the widening gap between rich and poor countries.

In addition to this, People in the global South have their consumption curtailed such that labour, resources and goods are more readily available for appropriation by Northern states and firms. This dynamic also helps us understand persistent inequality between the core and periphery.

The scale of value transfer has increased dramatically over time, driven by a concerted imperialist assault on the third world. The IMF, World Bank, and WTO have pressured Southern countries to deregulate labour markets, cut public sector employment, and eliminate tariffs, decimating their indigenous-controlled industrial sectors.

Key Statistics:
  • The South's annual losses from unequal exchange amount to 5.2% of its GDP.
  • The US gains $2,634 per person through unequal exchange, while the average Australian citizen receives $3,116.
  • In 2021, 9.6 trillion hours of labour went into producing for the global economy. Of that, 90% was contributed by the global South.
